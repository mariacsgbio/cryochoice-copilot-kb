1. Sperm & Fertility Biology Basics

Foundational context for anything else in this document — useful when a client asks something more basic than a report number, e.g. "how is sperm even made" or "why does age matter."

1.1 What sperm is, and how it's made

  • Sperm is the male sex cell ("gamete"). It's haploid — it carries 23 chromosomes, half the 46 found in a typical human cell. The egg carries the other 23; together at fertilization they make a diploid cell with the full 46.

  • Production ("spermatogenesis") starts at puberty, generally continues uninterrupted for life, and takes about 74 days from start to a fully mature sperm cell. A healthy male reproductive system produces roughly 50–100 million viable sperm per day.

  • Sperm are produced in the testes, inside structures called seminiferous tubules, through several rounds of division (germ cell → spermatocyte → spermatid → mature spermatozoa). Relatively cool temperatures help this process, which is why the testes hang outside the body, running about 4°F cooler than core body temperature.

1.2 Sperm anatomy

  • Head — houses the nucleus (the genetic material) and the acrosome, a cap of enzymes at the very tip that lets the sperm digest its way through the outer layer of the egg.

  • Midpiece ("neck" or "body") — packed with mitochondria that generate the energy for movement.

  • Tail — made of protein fibers that contract to propel the sperm forward.

1.3 Where sperm is stored, and the journey to fertilization

  • After production, sperm mature and are stored in the epididymis (specifically its "cauda" section), a coiled tube behind each testicle. During ejaculation, muscle contractions move them through the vas deferens, where they mix with fluid from the seminal vesicles to form semen, then out through the urethra.

  • Fertilization is a long-odds journey: of the roughly 80–300 million sperm released per ejaculation, fewer than 1 in a million reach the egg. Most are eliminated by the vagina's acidic environment, trapped in cervical mucus "crypts," or lost heading up the wrong fallopian tube. The survivors undergo final chemical changes that let the acrosome break through the egg's outer layer.

1.4 Are sperm "alive"? How long do they last?

  • Sperm have some hallmarks of life — they metabolize energy, mature, and move on their own — but can't reproduce independently, since they carry only half the genetic material needed for a new cell.

  • Inside the body: sperm take about 70–90 days to be produced and mature in the testes/epididymis; once ejaculated into the female reproductive tract, they can survive roughly 5 days.

  • Outside the body: sperm survive only seconds to a few hours, depending on the surface, temperature, and humidity, unless properly preserved (as with CryoChoice's transport media or freezing).

1.5 What "sperm quality" means, and how it's tested

  • Sperm quality refers to a sample's ability to accomplish fertilization, assessed across three main parameters: motility (movement), morphology (shape/structure), and genetic/DNA health. Quantity (count/concentration) is a related but separate factor — see 1.6 below.

  • It's measured via a semen analysis (the standard test — examines concentration, motility, and morphology under a microscope) and, optionally, DNA fragmentation analysis for a deeper look at genetic health.

  • Testing can happen in-office (a fertility clinic or urologist's andrology lab — sample collected on-site, brought to the lab within 60 minutes) or at-home (CryoChoice's model — the sample is collected in a special buffering medium that stays viable for up to 48 hours, then shipped overnight to the lab). CryoChoice's kits test every parameter a clinic-based andrology lab would, not just the basic count/concentration some other mail-in kits limit themselves to.

1.6 Quality vs. quantity — they're not the same thing

Concentration is a density (sperm per mL). Total count is that density × the sample's volume. Example: 3 mL of semen at 15 million/mL = a total count of 45 million sperm. A high total count isn't very helpful if most of those sperm are misshapen, can't swim, or carry damaged DNA — quality and quantity issues can share the same causes (toxin exposure, smoking, hormone imbalance) but are measured, and can move, independently of each other.

1.7 What affects sperm quality

  • Age: sperm motility declines roughly 0.8%/year on average, normal morphology declines 0.2–0.9%/year, and DNA fragmentation and new genetic mutations both rise gradually with age.

  • Lifestyle: sleep, smoking, drug and alcohol use, diet, exercise, body weight, stress, tight/hot underwear, and frequent hot tub use can all affect motility, morphology, and DNA fragmentation. Because the body is constantly making new sperm, a positive lifestyle change can show measurable improvement in as little as 2–3 months.

  • Medical history: testosterone replacement therapy, anabolic steroids, antidepressants, chemotherapy, opiates, and NSAIDs can all impact sperm quality/production — reversibility depends on the specific drug, dose, and duration. A recent infection, illness, or fever can also temporarily lower sperm quality.

  • Toxin exposure: lead, pesticides, hydrocarbons, PCBs, cadmium, diesel exhaust, petrochemicals, and solvents are all linked to reduced sperm quality — exposure is roughly twice as common among infertile men as among the general population. (See Section 7 for occupations most affected.)

1.8 Why male fertility testing matters

  • Male fertility issues contribute to 30–50% of diagnosed infertility cases — at least one in three.

  • Average sperm concentration has fallen by roughly 50% since 1973 (from about 99 million/mL to about 47 million/mL, per published research).

  • Sperm accumulates new genetic mutations over a man's life — roughly one new mutation every 8 months — which is part of why age matters for fertility planning, not just for the mother's age.

1.9 When a client should see a specialist

Recommend a doctor's visit if: a semen analysis came back very abnormal (e.g. no sperm found at all), lifestyle changes haven't moved the numbers after a few months, or the couple hasn't achieved pregnancy after 6–12 months of trying. Clients would typically see either a reproductive endocrinologist (works with couples trying to conceive) or a urologist (treats male infertility and sexual health). No doctor's referral is required to purchase a CryoChoice kit or storage plan in the first place.

4. Complete Report Field Glossary

This section defines every field that appears on a CryoChoice fertility report — both the client-facing "Your Fertility Analysis" page and the "For Medical Professionals" detail page — in the order they appear on the report. This agent is internal-only (used by trained reps, never directly by clients), so the depth here is deliberately full: a rep should be able to look up literally any number or label a client reads off their own report, understand what it means, and know whether it's within the normal range — without needing to escalate just because the term sounds technical. 3.1 Processing & sample information

Field What it is Why it matters
Collection Date / Time When the client produced the sample. Used to calculate abstinence days and how long the sample was in transit before processing.
Processing Date / Time When the lab actually received and analyzed the sample. The gap between collection and processing should stay within the kit's viability window (CryoChoice's transport media keeps a sample viable up to 48 hours, vs. 60 minutes for a walk-in clinic sample) — a longer gap than expected is a reasonable question to ask about if a result looks off.
Condition Upon Arrival The lab's assessment of how the sample looked when it reached them (e.g. "Good"). Flags whether shipping/handling may have affected the sample before testing even started — relevant context if a client asks why a result seems worse than expected.
Days Abstinent Days since the client's last ejaculation before producing this sample. Too short an abstinence window can lower volume/concentration; too long can reduce motility. CryoChoice recommends 2–5 days, ideally 3.
Laboratory The accredited andrology lab that performed the analysis (e.g. CA Fertility Experts). May vary by kit/region; useful if a client or clinician asks who analyzed the sample.
Liquefaction Freshly ejaculated semen initially thickens, then liquefies within roughly 15–30 minutes due to prostate enzymes. "Normal" means this happened as expected. Abnormal (slow/incomplete) liquefaction can trap sperm and interfere with getting an accurate motility reading — it's a sample-quality factor, not a sperm-quality one.
Liquefaction Treatment Whether the lab had to add an enzyme to force liquefaction because the sample didn't liquefy on its own in time. "No" means the sample liquefied naturally; "Yes" means the lab intervened, which is useful context if paired with an unusual motility result.
Visual Appearance The general look of the sample (color/clarity). "Normal" = typical grayish-white, opalescent. Red/brown can indicate blood, yellow-green can suggest infection or certain medications/vitamins, and unusually clear can suggest low sperm count — this is descriptive, not diagnostic.
Viscosity How thick/sticky the liquefied sample is. "Normal" means it flows properly. Abnormally high viscosity can trap sperm and interfere with accurate motility/count testing, and can sometimes be linked to inflammation or infection.
pH Acidity/alkalinity of the semen. Normal is slightly alkaline (roughly 7.2–8.0); the sample report shows 7.4. An abnormal pH (too acidic or too alkaline) can point to a blockage, infection, or an issue with the seminal vesicles or prostate's contribution to the fluid.

3.2 Core fertility metrics (WHO 6th Edition reference values)

These are the headline numbers on both the client page and the detail page.

Parameter Lower/upper reference limit In plain language
Ejaculate volume ≥ 1.4 mL Total volume of the sample. Low volume can point to a structural or collection issue, not sperm quality itself.
Concentration ≥ 16 million/mL How densely packed sperm are in the fluid.
Total sperm number ≥ 39 million Concentration × volume — the total sperm in the whole sample, not just per mL.
Total motile count (TMC) > 40 million (a separate, widely-used clinical benchmark, not a WHO 6th line item) Total sperm number × % motile — the number of sperm that are actually moving, not just present. CryoChoice's own report calls this "the most important factor in determining fertility." If total motility is at or near 0%, TMC is reported as N/A rather than a near-zero number, since there aren't enough moving sperm to count reliably.
Total motility ≥ 42% % of sperm that are moving at all (any movement, useful or not).
Progressive motility ≥ 30% % moving usefully — in straight lines or large circles.
– Rapid progressive No separate WHO reference range — informational only The fastest-moving portion of the progressive group.
– Slow progressive No separate WHO reference range — informational only The slower-moving portion of the progressive group — still moving usefully, just not quickly.
Non-progressive motility ≥ 1% % moving, but not in a useful directional pattern (e.g. tight circles, vibrating in place).
Immotile < 58% % not moving at all.
Normal morphology ≥ 4% % of sperm with a normal shape/structure. Even healthy, fertile men often have only a small minority of "normal" sperm.

A quick way to keep Total Sperm Number, Total Motile Count, and Concentration straight: Concentration is a density (sperm per mL); Total Sperm Number is that density multiplied by the whole sample's volume (every sperm in the ejaculate, moving or not); Total Motile Count is Total Sperm Number narrowed down to only the ones actually moving. Three different numbers answering three different questions.

3.3 Concentration & morphology detail

Field What it measures
MSC (Motile Sperm Concentration) The concentration of just the moving sperm (concentration × % total motility).
PMSC (Progressive Motile Sperm Concentration) The concentration of sperm moving in a useful, progressive pattern (concentration × % progressive motility) — the most clinically relevant density figure for natural conception or IUI.
Abnormal Heads % of abnormal sperm whose defect is in the head (e.g. amorphous, pyriform/tapering, vacuolated, or oversized heads).
Abnormal Midpieces % of abnormal sperm with a defect in the midpiece/neck (e.g. bent or asymmetrical).
Abnormal Tails % of abnormal sperm with a tail defect (e.g. coiled, short, missing, or double tails).
Excess Residual Cytoplasm % of sperm still carrying leftover cytoplasm (cell material) that should have been shed during maturation — a sign of an immature or incompletely-matured sperm cell.
Teratozoospermia Index (TZI) The average number of defects (out of head/midpiece/tail/cytoplasm) found per abnormal sperm. A TZI closer to 1 means abnormal sperm tend to have just one defect; a higher TZI (up to 4) means abnormal sperm tend to have multiple defects at once.

Note: these four defect categories aren't mutually exclusive and don't need to add up to 100% — a single abnormal sperm can have, say, both an abnormal head and an abnormal tail, and would count toward both percentages.

3.4 The CASA kinematic values (LIN, VSL, STR, VCL, WOB, ALH, VAP, BCF)

These appear in the "Motility" detail grid on the "For Medical Professionals" page. They're produced automatically by the CASA (computer-assisted sperm analysis) system, which tracks each individual sperm head's position frame-by-frame and derives these values from that movement path.

Value Full name What it measures
VCL Curvilinear velocity Speed along the sperm's actual, wiggly path (μm/s) — the fastest-looking of the three velocity measures, since it follows every wobble.
VSL Straight-line velocity Speed measured as a straight line from the sperm's start point to its end point (μm/s) — ignores the wiggles entirely.
VAP Average path velocity Speed along a smoothed version of the path (μm/s) — splits the difference between VCL and VSL.
LIN Linearity VSL ÷ VCL, as a %. How close to a straight line the sperm's actual path is — 100% would be perfectly straight.
STR Straightness VSL ÷ VAP, as a %. Similar idea to linearity, but compared against the smoothed path rather than the raw one.
WOB Wobble VAP ÷ VCL, as a %. How much the sperm's real path oscillates around its own smoothed path.
ALH Amplitude of lateral head displacement How far side-to-side the sperm's head swings while it swims (μm).
BCF Beat cross frequency How many times per second the head crosses the sperm's average path line (Hz) — a proxy for tail-beat rate.

The small bar charts under VAP*, VSL*, STR*, and ALH* on the report are distribution histograms, not extra metrics — they show what percentage of individually-tracked sperm fell into each speed/displacement range (e.g. "100% of sperm were in the 0–5 μm/s bucket"), rather than just the single averaged number above. They're a more detailed picture of the same VAP/VSL/STR/ALH values, useful for spotting whether a sample's movement is fairly uniform or a mix of very different sperm — not something reps need to interpret line-by-line, but good to recognize if a client or clinician points at the chart.

Why every kinematic value and histogram can read 0% / 0.0 on a report: they're all calculations built on top of a moving sperm's tracked path. When total motility is at or near 0% (as in the sample report used to build this knowledge base — total motility <2%), there's no meaningful movement to measure, so every downstream kinematic value collapses to 0 and every histogram bucket piles into the lowest bin. That's an expected mathematical consequence of very low motility, not a separate abnormal finding or a lab error — reps can confidently explain that an all-zero motility grid is exactly what a very low motility result looks like on paper, not a sign the lab made a mistake.

3.5 Post-thaw analysis

Field What it means
Number of Vials Cryopreserved How many separate storage vials the lab was able to divide this sample into for freezing.
Concentration per Vial Sperm concentration in each individual vial after the sample is mixed with cryoprotectant and divided — usually lower than the original whole-sample concentration because of that dilution.
Post Thaw Concentration Concentration measured after thawing one test vial, used to estimate what the remaining stored vials will look like when they're eventually used.
Post Thaw Motility % of sperm still moving after that thaw test.

It's normal for concentration and motility to be lower post-thaw than pre-freeze; on average, about 80% of sperm survive the freeze-thaw process, and this normal decline does not meaningfully affect pregnancy rates when the sample is eventually used.

3.6 The "WHO 6th" and "[95% CI]" columns

The WHO 6th column is the lower (or upper, for Immotile) reference limit from the World Health Organization's 6th Edition (2021) laboratory manual — the threshold a reference population of fertile men scored above (or below) 95% of the time. The [95% CI] column is a confidence interval on that reference limit itself (a statistical margin of error from the underlying research), not a range describing this specific client's result. In other words: the CI describes how confident WHO's own researchers are in the cutoff number, not how confident the lab is in this client's test.

3.7 How CryoChoice frames the limits of this test (use this language)

Every report includes a limitations disclaimer — it's good, accurate language reps can echo when a client asks "how sure are we about this":

  • A semen analysis can't detect everything that affects fertility — infections, genetic disorders, vasectomy status, and anatomical issues like varicoceles need other tests if suspected.

  • Semen parameters vary from week to week, so one test cannot guarantee whether or not someone will be able to conceive naturally — a different result on a different day doesn't mean either result was "wrong."

  • A full clinical evaluation should involve the client's physician — this report is reference information, not a substitute for professional medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment.

5. Understanding an Abnormal Result — the conversation reps most often escalate

WHY THIS SECTION MATTERS — A client seeing very low numbers (e.g. <2% motility, 3% morphology, low concentration) is often frightened and asks a rep directly whether this means they can't have children. This is the single most common reason reps go straight to leadership. The talking points below let a rep respond calmly and correctly without making a medical judgment call themselves.

Core message: abnormal ≠ infertile

  • A very low or even 0% normal morphology result does not necessarily mean someone is infertile. In one study, nearly 30% of men with 0% normal morphology still achieved pregnancy with their partner without fertility treatment (vs. 55% of men with healthy morphology).

  • Semen analysis is one input among several factors affecting fertility — it doesn't capture everything (e.g. genetic factors, the partner's fertility).

  • Results can vary from week to week; a single test is a snapshot, not a permanent verdict.

If post-thaw motility comes back at or near 0%

  • This does NOT mean the sample should be discarded. Clients can still freeze/store a sample that shows 0% post-thaw motility.

  • Frozen sperm typically loses roughly 20–50% of its motility in the freeze-thaw process — a drop to 0% motility does not mean the sperm are dead.

  • Immotile sperm can still be used in IVF with ICSI (Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection), where an embryologist manually injects a single sperm into an egg — the sperm's inability to swim is irrelevant because it never needs to swim.

  • Embryologists distinguish alive-but-paralyzed sperm from dead sperm using techniques such as the Hypo-Osmotic Swelling (HOS) test, laser selection, or chemical stimulants (e.g. pentoxifylline).

  • Common causes of 0% post-thaw motility include: severe pre-freeze asthenozoospermia (already-low motility before freezing), immotile cilia syndrome (a structural defect where sperm are alive but can't move), or severe cryodamage in individuals whose sperm membranes don't tolerate freezing well.

  • Bottom line for the client: don't discard the sample without consulting a fertility specialist — a 0% post-thaw motility result is a hurdle for ICSI-based treatment, not an absolute barrier to biological fatherhood.

ESCALATE — What the rep should NOT do: tell the client what their odds of conceiving are, tell them whether they're "infertile," or recommend a specific treatment path (IUI vs. IVF vs. ICSI). Give the general context above, then route to a fertility specialist/clinician for anything that requires judgment about this specific client's case.

6. Freezing & Storage

Q: How is sperm frozen?

A: Sperm is mixed with a cryoprotectant (which draws water out of the cell to prevent ice-crystal damage), loaded into cryogenic vials, and plunged into liquid nitrogen at -196°C or lower, then moved to long-term liquid nitrogen storage tanks.

Q: How many vials should a client freeze?

A: Depends on family-planning goals and sperm quality after thaw. One vial ≈ one IUI attempt (10–20% success rate per attempt) — recommend 3–5 vials per pregnancy hoped for via IUI. IVF/ICSI can use a single vial many times over, since one vial holds hundreds of thousands to millions of sperm.

Q: How long can sperm stay frozen?

A: Essentially indefinitely under proper storage conditions. Studies show no meaningful effect on birth rates for the first 5 years of storage; a small decrease in survival rate after 5 years hasn't been shown to significantly affect clinical outcomes even out to 11–15 years.

Q: How and where is frozen sperm stored?

A: In cryogenic vials within liquid nitrogen or nitrogen-vapor tanks ("dewars"), at a dedicated, HIPAA-compliant cryogenic storage facility with 24/7 monitoring, backup power, and card-controlled access.

Q: What are the options for using frozen sperm later?

A: Intrauterine insemination (IUI, needs total motile sperm count typically above ~8 million post-thaw), in vitro fertilization (IVF), and IVF with ICSI (for low motility/count cases). Pregnancy rates using frozen sperm are comparable to fresh sperm across all three.

7. Special Situations

Vasectomy & vasectomy reversal

  • Post-vasectomy: get a semen analysis around 12 weeks after the procedure to confirm it worked.

  • Post-vasectomy-reversal: retest monthly for 12 months to confirm sperm count/quality is recovering, since it can take 6–12 months to start producing sperm again.

  • Freezing sperm before a vasectomy is a lower-cost, less invasive backup plan than a future vasectomy reversal (reversal success rates are typically cited as 80–90% with an experienced microsurgeon, but pregnancy-rate estimates after reversal can be as low as 30%, and antisperm antibodies are present in 53% of men who've had a vasectomy).

Cancer patients

  • Up to 75% of cancer patients may have impaired fertility as a result of treatment (chemotherapy, radiation, surgery, bone marrow transplant); some of this damage is irreversible.

  • Sperm freezing before treatment starts is the standard recommendation. Route these clients to 866-308-3085 for discount/process details.

Military & high-risk occupations

  • Veterans show meaningfully higher infertility rates than civilians in multiple studies; occupational exposure to toxins (agriculture, herbicides, construction, machining, plastics, printing, tobacco processing, welding, ionizing radiation) is also linked to reduced sperm quality.

  • These clients may want to freeze proactively before deployment or before/during high-exposure work.

Age & family planning later in life

  • Sperm quality changes gradually with age: roughly -0.8%/year motility, -0.65%/year normal morphology, +0.3%/year DNA fragmentation, and about 1.5 new genetic mutations per year of a man's life.

  • Fertility preservation in one's 20s–30s is commonly framed as "insurance" for having children later, since sperm quality after 35 shows a measurable decline in pregnancy rate and a rise in miscarriage risk.
